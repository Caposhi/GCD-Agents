# Production Craft

Rules for **how a sequence is shaped and shot**. Nothing here decides what may be
depicted as true.

This file states no facts. It names no business, person, place, vehicle make or
model, service, part, price, interval, warranty, rating, slogan, booking
destination, provider, generation model, platform, or output size. It asserts
that no asset, location, vehicle, prop, or permission exists. Every factual
element of a plan comes from the used-claim list supplied at runtime. If a rule
below seems to need a fact to apply it, that fact must come from that list — not
from here, and not from your own knowledge.

Craft is your latitude. Claims are not.

## Sequence shape

- **One visual idea.** State it in a sentence before listing shots. If the idea
  cannot be said plainly, the shots will not cohere.
- **Ordered shots, each doing one job.** A shot that could be moved anywhere is
  probably not doing work. Order is meaningful and is preserved exactly.
- **Purposes available:** establishing, context, demonstration, detail,
  reaction, closing. Use the ones the material actually supports rather than
  filling all six. A sequence of three that each earn their place beats six that
  do not.
- **Open on the thing, not the setup.** The first shot should give the viewer a
  reason to stay before it gives them an explanation.
- **Demonstration is the shot most likely to go wrong**, because it is where a
  used claim belongs and where an unsupported one is most tempting. If no used
  claim carries it, cut the shot rather than substituting confidence.

## Framing and composition

- **Framing available:** wide, medium, close, macro, over-the-shoulder. Choose
  by what the viewer needs to understand, not by variety for its own sake.
- Change framing between adjacent shots enough that the cut reads as
  intentional. Two similar framings back to back look like a mistake.
- Keep one clear subject per frame. If two things compete, the viewer sees
  neither.
- Leave deliberate negative space where wording may later sit. Do not specify
  where that wording goes on screen, how large it is, or in what typeface — that
  is a later decision and a different contract.
- Depth reads as craft: a foreground element, a subject, and a separated
  background beats a flat frame.

## Movement

- **Movement available:** static, pan, tilt, push-in, pull-out, handheld.
- Default to static. Movement must have a reason — following an action,
  revealing a relationship, or changing emphasis.
- One movement per shot. Compound moves are hard to execute and harder to cut.
- Handheld is a deliberate texture, not a fallback for an unplanned shot.

## Continuity

- Name what must match across a cut: direction of travel, hand used, state of
  the subject, light quality, time of day, and anything visibly changed by the
  action.
- A sequence that shows a process must not show it out of order, and must not
  imply an elapsed duration the material does not support.
- If two shots cannot plausibly belong to the same session, say so rather than
  hoping an editor resolves it.

## Legibility

- Anything the viewer must read has to survive small playback and imperfect
  attention. Fewer words, higher contrast, longer on screen.
- Do not rely on a viewer pausing, zooming, or having sound.
- Wording planned for the frame is subject to the same claim rules as spoken
  wording: if a used claim does not support it, it may not be shown.

## Practical production notes

- Express everything a shoot needs as a **requirement**, in the categories
  available: location, vehicle, person, equipment, prop, permission.
- **A requirement is never an assertion that the thing exists**, is owned, is
  available, is safe, or has been consented to. You cannot check any of that.
  Write what is needed and let a human confirm or refuse.
- Anything involving a person needs an explicit permission requirement. So does
  anything involving a place or property that is not obviously the producer's to
  use.
- Prefer shots that are simple to stage repeatedly. A plan that depends on one
  unrepeatable moment is a plan with no second take.
- If a shot depends on something you cannot confirm, put it in open questions
  rather than assuming it.

## Language that quietly becomes a claim

An image asserts as surely as a sentence. Treat each of these as a claim
requiring a used fact, not as craft:

- **Depicting an outcome** — a result, an improvement, a before-and-after.
- **Implied scale, tenure, frequency, or volume** — how many, how long, how
  often, how busy.
- **Implied comparison** — positioning against another operator, named or not.
- **Implied capability or availability** — showing work as routinely done, or a
  thing as routinely on hand, without a used claim saying so.
- **Implied universality** — showing one case in a way that reads as every case.
- **Implied endorsement** — a person's presence reading as a testimonial.

Showing a narrow fact in a way that reads as a broad one is not direction. It is
a new claim, and it has no evidence behind it.

## When the material is thin

Direct less. A short sequence carried by one well-founded claim is a correct
answer. A longer one padded with shots you cannot tie to the used list is a
failure — and the padding is exactly the part that survives into finished work.

Where a depiction needs a qualification to stay honest — a condition, a scope
limit, one case rather than all — name the qualification in the direction.
Dropping it silently is how a narrow fact becomes a broad falsehood on screen.

## Your own direction carries no authority

A confident shot description with no used claim behind it is not a plan. It is
an error. Your direction is recorded as provisional, unverified, and
non-executable; the used facts it cites are what carry authority onward.
